2.Task 01 — Cybersecurity and Cyber Law: A Short Note

2.1 What is Cybersecurity

Cybersecurity is the practice of protecting computer systems, networks, programs, devices and data from unauthorised access, damage, disruption, alteration or theft. As almost every part of modern life — banking, education, healthcare, government services, business and personal communication — now depends on digital systems, protecting those systems has become as important as protecting physical property.

Cybersecurity is not a single product that can be purchased and installed. It is a continuous process built on three pillars: people (awareness and trained professionals), process (policies, standards and incident-response procedures) and technology (firewalls, encryption, monitoring and access control). A weakness in any one pillar can compromise the whole system, which is why attackers so often succeed through social engineering rather than through technical flaws.

2.2 The CIA Triad — the Core Principles

Every security control ultimately serves one or more of the three principles known as the CIA Triad. These three goals form the foundation of all cybersecurity work.

PrincipleMeaningExample of Protection
ConfidentialityInformation is accessible only to those who are authorised to see it.Encryption, strong passwords, access control lists, multi-factor authentication
IntegrityInformation remains accurate, complete and unaltered by unauthorised parties.Hashing (SHA-256), digital signatures, checksums, file-integrity monitoring
AvailabilitySystems and data are accessible to authorised users whenever required.Backups, redundancy, DDoS protection, disaster-recovery planning

Two further principles are frequently added in practice: Authentication (proving that a user is who they claim to be) and Non-repudiation (ensuring a party cannot later deny an action they performed).

2.6 What is Cyber Law

Cyber law (also called IT law or digital law) is the body of legislation that governs the use of computers, the internet, digital data and electronic communication. It defines what constitutes a cyber offence, prescribes punishments, establishes investigative authority, and creates rights and obligations in the digital space.

The main objectives of cyber law are to:

  • Criminalise unauthorised access, data theft, hacking and digital fraud;
  • Give legal recognition to electronic records, digital signatures and electronic contracts;
  • Protect personal data, privacy and intellectual property online;
  • Protect critical information infrastructure such as banking, power and telecommunication systems;
  • Establish procedures for investigation, digital evidence and prosecution of cybercrime.
Why cyber law matters to an ethical hacker The technical steps performed by a penetration tester and by a criminal attacker can be almost identical — scanning, enumeration, exploitation. The only thing that separates a professional from a criminal is documented authorisation and an agreed scope. Knowing the law is therefore not optional background knowledge; it is what makes the profession lawful.

2.7 Cyber Law in Bangladesh

Bangladesh has developed its cyber legislation in several stages. The table below summarises the main instruments and how they relate to one another.

LegislationYearScope and Status
Information & Communication Technology (ICT) Act2006
(amended 2009, 2013)		Bangladesh's first comprehensive ICT statute. Gave legal recognition to electronic records and digital signatures and criminalised hacking, unauthorised access and digital forgery. Its controversial speech provisions (notably s. 57) were later removed and carried into subsequent laws.
Pornography Control Act2012Prohibits production and distribution of pornographic material, including in digital form; frequently applied in online blackmail and revenge-content cases.
Digital Security Act (DSA)2018Consolidated cybercrime offences, established the Digital Security Agency and a national CERT, and covered illegal access, identity fraud, cyber-terrorism and offences against critical information infrastructure. Widely criticised on freedom-of-expression grounds; subsequently repealed.
Cyber Security Act (CSA)2023Enacted to replace the Digital Security Act 2018. Retained the core cybercrime offences while reducing several penalties and making some offences bailable.
Cyber Protection / Cyber Security Ordinance2025Promulgated by the interim government to replace the Cyber Security Act 2023, repealing several speech-related provisions while retaining offences relating to hacking, unauthorised access, digital fraud and online harassment.
Digital Commerce & consumer protection rulesvariousSupplementary instruments governing e-commerce, online payment and consumer redress.
Note on currency of the law Bangladesh's cyber legislation has changed repeatedly between 2018 and 2025. Before relying on any specific section number or penalty in professional work, the current consolidated text should be verified from the official gazette or from the Bangladesh Legislative and Parliamentary Affairs Division website (bdlaws.minlaw.gov.bd).

Categories of offence recognised under Bangladeshi cyber legislation

  • Illegal access — entering a computer, network or digital device without authorisation.
  • Damage to computer or computer system — deleting, altering or disabling data or systems.
  • Offences against Critical Information Infrastructure — attacks on systems declared critical by the government (banking, power, telecom); these attract the heaviest penalties.
  • Identity fraud and impersonation — using another person's identity or credentials online.
  • Digital forgery and digital fraud — falsifying electronic records for gain.
  • Cyber-terrorism — attacks intended to threaten sovereignty, public safety or national integrity.
  • Publication of defamatory, obscene or offensive material in electronic form.
  • Online harassment, cyber-bullying and blackmail, including non-consensual image sharing.

Where to report cybercrime in Bangladesh

2.9 Legal Boundaries of an Ethical Hacker

An ethical hacker performs the same technical activities as an attacker, but under a legal framework. The following conditions must always be satisfied before any testing begins:

  1. Written authorisation. A signed engagement letter, contract or "rules of engagement" document from the legitimate owner of the system. Verbal permission is never sufficient.
  2. Defined scope. Exact IP addresses, domains, applications and time windows that may be tested. Anything outside the scope must not be touched.
  3. Non-Disclosure Agreement (NDA). Any data discovered during testing is confidential and must not be copied, retained or disclosed.
  4. No harm principle. Testing must avoid denial of service, data destruction or modification unless explicitly permitted in writing.
  5. Responsible disclosure. Findings are reported privately to the owner with sufficient time to remediate before any public disclosure.
  6. Accurate reporting. Full documentation of methodology, evidence, risk rating and remediation advice.

Practising on systems that are not one's own and for which no permission exists is a criminal offence. For lawful practice, ethical hackers use their own laboratory environments (virtual machines such as Metasploitable, DVWA, OWASP Juice Shop) or authorised legal platforms such as TryHackMe, Hack The Box, PortSwigger Web Security Academy and public bug-bounty programmes operating under a published safe-harbour policy.

2.10 Summary of Task 01

Cybersecurity protects the confidentiality, integrity and availability of digital assets through a combination of people, process and technology. Cyber law supplies the legal structure that defines digital offences and legitimises defensive work. For a student of ethical hacking, the two subjects are inseparable: technical capability without legal authorisation is a crime, while legal knowledge without technical skill cannot defend a system. Understanding both is the foundation on which the rest of this course is built.

3.Task 02 — Installation of Kali Linux and VMware

3.1 Why Kali Linux

Kali Linux is a Debian-based Linux distribution maintained by Offensive Security and built specifically for penetration testing, digital forensics and security auditing. It ships with several hundred pre-installed security tools — including Nmap, Metasploit Framework, Burp Suite, Wireshark, Aircrack-ng, John the Ripper, Hydra and sqlmap — which removes the need to install and configure each tool individually. It is the standard working environment for this course.

3.2 Virtualization and VMware

Virtualization allows one physical computer (the host) to run one or more complete operating systems (guests) simultaneously inside isolated software containers called virtual machines. VMware Workstation Player is a widely used desktop hypervisor for this purpose.

Running Kali Linux inside a virtual machine offers several advantages for a learner:

  • Isolation — testing activity is confined to the VM and cannot damage the host operating system.
  • Snapshots — the machine state can be saved before a risky operation and restored instantly if something breaks.
  • Coexistence — Kali runs alongside Windows without repartitioning the disk or dual-booting.
  • Lab networking — multiple VMs (for example Kali and a vulnerable target such as Metasploitable) can be placed on a private host-only network, allowing lawful practice against a target you own.

3.3 Method A — Kali Linux on VMware Workstation Player

The following is the standard procedure for building the virtual laboratory, as demonstrated in the class and on the Mission Hackers Bangladesh YouTube channel.

Step 1 — Verify hardware and enable virtualization

  • Minimum practical requirements: 64-bit processor, 8 GB RAM (4 GB absolute minimum), 40–60 GB free disk space.
  • Reboot into BIOS/UEFI and enable Intel VT-x or AMD-V. Without this, the VM will not start or will run extremely slowly.
  • On Windows hosts, disabling Hyper-V / "Memory Integrity" is often required for VMware to obtain hardware acceleration.

Step 2 — Install VMware Workstation Player

  • Download VMware Workstation Player from the official Broadcom/VMware website only.
  • Run the installer, accept the licence, keep the default installation path and complete the setup.
  • Reboot the host and launch the application, selecting the free non-commercial use option.

Step 3 — Obtain the Kali Linux image

  • Download from the official source: https://www.kali.org/get-kali/
  • Two options are available:
    • Pre-built VMware image (recommended) — a ready-made .7z archive containing a .vmx file. Extract it and open the .vmx directly in VMware; no OS installation is needed.
    • Installer ISO — a full .iso image used to create and install a new virtual machine manually.
  • Verify the SHA-256 checksum of the download against the value published on kali.org before use. This confirms the image has not been corrupted or tampered with.

Step 4 — Create the virtual machine (ISO method)

  • Create a New Virtual Machine → select Installer disc image file (iso) and browse to the Kali ISO.
  • Guest operating system: Linux → version Debian 12.x 64-bit.
  • Name the machine (e.g. Kali-Linux-Batch11) and choose a storage location on a drive with sufficient free space.
  • Disk size: 60 GB, stored as a single file.
  • Customize Hardware: Memory 4096 MB (8192 MB if the host has 16 GB), Processors 2 cores, Network Adapter set to NAT for internet access, or Host-only for an isolated lab.
  • Finish and power on the virtual machine.

Step 5 — Install the Kali operating system

  • Select Graphical Install from the boot menu.
  • Choose language, location and keyboard layout.
  • Set the hostname (e.g. kali), leave the domain blank.
  • Create the user account and a strong password.
  • Partitioning: Guided — use entire disk and All files in one partition. This affects only the virtual disk, not the host machine.
  • Select the desktop environment (XFCE is the default and lightest) and the default tool collection.
  • Install the GRUB boot loader to the virtual disk (/dev/sda).
  • Complete the installation and reboot into the new system.

Step 6 — Post-installation of the guest

  • Update the system: sudo apt update && sudo apt full-upgrade -y
  • Install guest tools for clipboard sharing and automatic screen resizing: sudo apt install -y open-vm-tools open-vm-tools-desktop
  • Reboot, then take a clean snapshot named "Fresh Install" so the machine can always be restored to a working state.

3.4 Method B — Native Installation (Setup Used by Me)

In addition to studying the virtual-machine procedure described above, I have installed Kali Linux directly on my laptop as the primary operating system (bare-metal installation). This gives full hardware access — which is particularly useful for wireless adapter work later in the course — at the cost of the snapshot and isolation benefits that a virtual machine provides.

The native installation procedure I followed was:

  1. Downloaded the official Kali Linux Installer ISO from kali.org/get-kali and verified its SHA-256 checksum.
  2. Wrote the ISO to a USB flash drive to create a bootable installation medium.
  3. Backed up all existing personal data before proceeding.
  4. Entered the UEFI/BIOS setup, disabled Secure Boot and set the USB device as the first boot option.
  5. Booted the installer and selected Graphical Install.
  6. Configured locale, keyboard layout, hostname and user account.
  7. Partitioned the internal disk and installed the base system together with the KDE Plasma desktop environment and the default Kali tool set.
  8. Installed the GRUB boot loader and rebooted into the installed system.
  9. Ran the first full update and configured the working environment.
